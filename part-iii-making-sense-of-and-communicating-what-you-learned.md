# Part III: Making Sense of and Communicating What You Learned

The Introduction, Background, and Methods provide the foundation for your research. During IQP, you will collect and analyze your data and use that evidence to answer your research questions. Your final report adds three chapters that communicate what you learned from that research, what you recommend based on what you learned, and what readers should ultimately take away from the project.

## 3.1 Findings

<strong>Purpose:</strong> Explain what your research helped you learn and support those findings with evidence from your data.

Topics to develop in this section:

- the difference between <strong>data and findings</strong>;
- moving from analysis to findings;
- organizing Findings around what you learned rather than around individual methods;
- making claims and supporting them with evidence;
- using qualitative and quantitative evidence effectively;
- using triangulation to strengthen, complicate, or qualify findings;
- connecting findings back to research questions;
- acknowledging contradictory or unexpected evidence;
- incorporating research limitations where they affect how a finding should be interpreted;
- using figures, tables, quotations, and other evidence to support the narrative; and
- distinguishing Findings from Background and Methods.

<strong>Key distinction to develop:</strong>

<strong>Background:</strong> What was already known?<br><strong>Methods:</strong> What did you do to investigate your questions?<br><strong>Findings:</strong> What did your research help you learn?

## 3.2 Recommendations

<strong>Purpose:</strong> Explain what should happen as a result of what you learned through your research.

Topics to develop in this section:

- developing recommendations from findings and evidence;
- making the connection between <strong>Finding → Conclusion/Interpretation → Recommendation</strong> visible;
- identifying who each recommendation is for;
- making recommendations specific, realistic, and actionable;
- distinguishing evidence-based recommendations from ideas or suggestions;
- prioritizing recommendations when appropriate;
- recognizing when the evidence does not support a strong recommendation;
- recommendations for the project host, community, or other stakeholders; and
- recommendations for additional research or future work.

<strong>Key principle to develop:</strong>

A recommendation should not surprise the reader. The reader should be able to trace it back to your findings and the evidence that supports them.

## 3.3 Conclusion

<strong>Purpose:</strong> Provide closure to the completed project and help readers understand its larger significance, implications, and possible future direction.

Topics to develop in this section:

- drawing the report to a close without simply repeating Findings and Recommendations;
- briefly returning to what the project set out to accomplish;
- considering what the project contributed;
- identifying who may benefit from the work;
- considering broader implications or applications;
- identifying important questions or opportunities that remain;
- looking toward what might happen <strong>because of the research</strong>; and
- using an <strong>inverse-funnel structure</strong> where appropriate, moving from the specific project toward its broader significance.

<strong>Key distinction to develop:</strong>

<strong>Proposal Conclusion:</strong> What comes next <strong>in</strong> the research?<br><strong>Final Report Conclusion:</strong> What comes next <strong>because of</strong> the research?
